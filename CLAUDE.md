## Working rules (Lorcan's)
- Before any task, show a checklist of steps with a predicted timeline, and tick steps off as you go.
- **Accuracy first** (as close to 100% as possible; he accepts about 1 wrong in 100 for a much higher answer rate), then speed. End goal: every chocolate recognised correctly in under ~3 s.
- The scanner decides by itself: no tap-to-confirm. Keep the short hold-over-the-chocolate scan.
- **Fair testing rule:** every change is measured on held-back data it has never seen (`training/sessions.json`: test-4, test-5 and test-6 held back; test-3 is in the gallery). Report % right when answering, % answered and the confusion list. Never keep a change that makes the held-back result worse. Rules chosen by looking at a test session must be confirmed on the next one.
- Every test session gets saved (`tests/test-N/` and in `~/Chocolate Scanner`), and the model should learn from mistakes in them.
- Client page look stays luxury and matches zchocolat.com (Cormorant Garamond + Jost, black and white, square edges, official logo and photos). New testing tools go on the admin page only. Everything runs on the phone.
- After changing app files, bump `VERSION` in `app/sw.js` (and `MODEL_VERSION` only if a model file changed). After editing `app/index.html`, run `python3 tools/build-admin.py`.
- Run `tools/check.sh` (add `--browser` for the browser test) before Lorcan pushes. Commit each stage separately with a clear message.
- English first; French later if needed.

- **MobileNetV3-Small backbone swap, tried and blocked (30 Oct):** the object-recognition research flagged this as a possible way to make the shared backbone (used by the reader, chocoprint and orientation models) smaller and a touch faster — not more accurate. Before committing the ~3.5 hours of laptop time a real retrain of all three models would take, checked first whether a working MobileNetV3-Small backbone could even be produced in the old file format this project's code loads (`tf.loadLayersModel`, the same format `app/vendor/mnv2` is in). It can't, two different ways: Keras's standard MobileNetV3Small compiles its hard-swish/squeeze-excite math down to an op (`TFOpLambda`) the phone-side TFJS library doesn't know how to load at all. Its "minimalistic" variant (plain ReLU, no squeeze-excite, built to dodge exactly that problem) does load, but the conversion tool itself silently drops every single BatchNorm layer's weights in the process (136 of 170 missing) — a real bug in the converter, not a settings mistake. Stopped there rather than hand-building the whole architecture from scratch to work around a third-party tool's bug, since the win on offer was a smaller download, not better answers. No training time was spent — this was caught at the first, cheap step.
- **Continued 2 Nov, properly tested this time before building anything:** found a different TFJS file format — "graph model" instead of the old "layers model" — that CAN load the full MobileNetV3Small variant, hard-swish/squeeze-excite and all; confirmed it loads and predicts correctly (`tf.loadGraphModel`, 42ms for a batch of 2). That looked like it unblocked the swap for real. But a graph model can only run predictions — TFJS can't fine-tune one, unlike today's `app/vendor/mnv2` backbone. Checked why that matters before spending the ~3+ hours a full pipeline rewrite (new training scripts, a shared-backbone live app, a full retrain) would take: all three real training scripts (`train_reader.js`, `train_print.js`, `train_quarter.js`) deliberately fine-tune the *entire* backbone after a short warm-up, not just a small head on top — that's the actual recipe, not an accident. So before building, ran two cheap checks on the same 85 held-back test-4/5/6 photos instead of the full rewrite:
  - Frozen MobileNetV2 0.35 (today's backbone, un-fine-tuned) + a small trained head: 17/85 right (20.0%).
  - Frozen MobileNetV3-Small-full (the new graph model) + the same small head: 25/85 right (29.4%) — genuinely better raw features, as the research predicted.
  - The real, currently-deployed reader (MobileNetV2 0.35, actually fine-tuned, no shortcuts): 44/85 right (51.8%).

  Fine-tuning is worth +32 points over a frozen backbone; swapping to the better frozen backbone was only worth +9. Since the only working way to use MobileNetV3-Small-full in TFJS (the graph-model format) can't be fine-tuned at all, switching to it would trade away a much bigger gain for a much smaller one — very likely a net loss, not a win, despite MobileNetV3's raw features testing better in isolation. **Rejected on real numbers, not just a format problem this time. MobileNetV2 0.35 stays the backbone, fine-tuned per model exactly as today**; nothing in the live app or training pipeline changed. Would need either a size-matched MobileNetV3 variant that's actually fine-tunable (none converts cleanly today) or a different method entirely before this is worth revisiting.

- **General-project research, then a gyroscope tilt hint shipped (3 Nov):** asked to look wider than just the MobileNetV2/V3 backbone question — "how to make this work definitively" overall. That, plus a real precedent (a 2019 paper on reading embossed marks on 3D-printed objects from varied camera angles via synthetic training) pointed at the best-grounded next lever for tilt specifically — see Next steps.

## Known weak spots
- **Camera tilt (viewing angle, not rotation):** confirmed weak, with real evidence now (28 Oct), and now also
  confirmed hard to fix cheaply (29 Oct - see "Tilt-correction attempt" above). What first looked like a separate
  "N°5 magnet" problem in test-6 (zdark, N°1, N°8 and N°21 all wrongly matching N°5's chocoprint) turned out, on
  inspecting the actual saved photos, to be four badly angled, near-side-on shots — not an N°5 reference problem
  at all. Only handles roughly-overhead shots well. Tilt augmentation for the chocoprint (train a model to be
  tilt-invariant from examples) and, now, two different code-only correction attempts (mild shear guesses; real
  quadrilateral detection and unwarp) have all been tried and rejected — not currently used. Across 87 real test
  photos, neither correction attempt ever once fixed a wrong answer, while both added real risk to already-good
  photos — evidence the genuinely bad angled shots may be physically self-occluded (same reason Face ID fails at
  extreme head poses), not just mis-processed. The realistic goal remains "any angle a person could still read it
  at by eye", not literally any angle. Next lever, if picked up again, needs either a genuinely different method
  (not a variation on the two already tried) or a proper angled-photo dataset to check against - test-6's four
  real examples (`tests/test-6/closeup/{zdark,1,8,21}/`) were enough to catch an actively broken corner-detector,
  but not enough to tell whether any fix was actually working, versus just not breaking things. A gyroscope-based
  tilt hint shipped 3 Nov (see above) — it's a real-time nudge to avoid bad angles in the first place, using the
  phone's own sensor instead of guessing from the photo, but it doesn't change what the recognizer can handle once
  a frame is already tilted, and the 25° nudge threshold is still unvalidated against real sessions.

    **The denser-oblique-coverage half failed badly, in a new and specific way.** A properly held-out azimuth check (7.5° offset from the training grid, elevation 55° never trained at all) found: 70° dropped 70/80->45/80, 55° dropped 45/80->19/80, 40° tied at 11/80, 25° gained a little (0/80->8/80). **Overall 126/320 (old) vs 83/320 (new) - a dramatic regression, not a wash.** Tracing the actual wrong guesses found a clear, systematic bias: dozens of genuinely different chocolates (0, 1, 6, 9, 13, 16, 19, zwhite, 4) collapsed toward guessing **"7" or "9"** specifically under oblique views - too consistent to be noise. Most likely cause: the extra training weight given to protect the lookalike pairs (strongest for the already-fragile 4/5/6/7/9 cluster) appears to have destabilised that same cluster's embedding specifically under oblique viewing - exactly the condition this whole exercise targets. The real-photo fair-test above didn't catch this because real test photos are mostly near-overhead; only the genuinely held-out oblique check exposed it.
    **Verdict: not shipped, per the project's own rule - second clean "no" in a row, but with a diagnosable, specific cause this time** rather than a vague regression. If a third attempt is ever wanted, the lead is clear: the lookalike-protection weight needs to be checked against the oblique held-out test too, not just the real-photo one - a change can look like a clear win on one axis and a serious regression on the other, and only checking both together (as finally done here) catches it. All data, models and full diagnostics remain in the scratch copy, not this repo.
    **Decision (10 Nov, Lorcan's call): stop retraining for tilt.** Two multi-hour attempts both failed, and the second found a specific, real cause (steep angles are a genuine ceiling with this synthetic-data method) rather than just bad luck - not worth a third costly attempt on the same approach. Steep-angle recognition stays a known, accepted limit; the gyroscope nudge shipped 3 Nov (see "Known weak spots") remains the live mitigation - it steers people away from bad angles before scanning rather than trying to recognise through them. Revisit only if a genuinely different method turns up, or if real client failures from bad angles start showing up in practice.

9. **Finder-at-distance idea: cut-paste synthetic backgrounds — demo-scale trial run (9 Nov), tried properly and rejected.** Run as a three-stage research → code → test pipeline (not Lorcan's time) on its own scratch branch (`cutpaste-finder`), `main` left untouched throughout.
   - **Research found the real bottleneck before any code was written**: plenty of white-card chocolate close-ups exist to cut out (≈1,736 across all 28 chocolates, no shortage there), but genuinely real, non-white-card, non-held-back *background* photos are scarce — effectively only two on disk: the 45 `training-photos/none/` shots (plain wood grain, nothing else ever in frame) and 15 photos from the 3 Nov vision-chat experiment (all one sitting: same laptop-edge + desk, different chocolate each time). Neither contains the actual clutter (a keyboard, a hand, a bag) that test-9/10/11 showed fooling the live finder. Recommended a demo batch of 150–300 composites rather than the full ~2,000, since multiplying two textures wouldn't add real diversity.
   - **Built**: `tools/cutpaste_finder.js` (threshold-segments a chocolate off its white card, pastes it with a blurred edge onto one of the two real backgrounds plus one distractor chocolate, 300 composites) and `training/train_finder.js` (new from scratch — none had ever existed in this repo). Trained a demo finder on real + the 300 synthetic images, saved to `experiments/finder-new/` — **never touched `app/model6` or anything the live app loads.**
   - **Fair-tested properly, old finder vs new, before keeping anything** — and found a real problem with the usual tool along the way: `tools/fair_check.sh` can't actually swap the finder model (held-back sessions' `closeup/` photos were already cropped by whichever finder was live at capture time, so it was comparing nothing). Worked around it with one-off scripts that run the finder for real against the raw `camera/` frames.
   - **Result: a clean, consistent "no" on both axes — not just inconclusive.**
     - Finder hit-rate (does the box land on the real chocolate?) on the actual distance/clutter sessions this was built for: test-9 **regressed**, 8/9→6/9; test-10 and test-11 stayed at **0% for both old and new** (including still failing the one deliberate "nothing in frame" shot) — zero improvement on the keyboard-lock case specifically.
     - Full end-to-end accuracy on the ordinary held-back sessions it's supposed to leave alone: test-4/5/6/7 combined **90/112 (80.4%) → 87/112 (77.7%)** — worse on 3 of 4, tied on the 4th, improved on none.
   - **Verdict, matching the research pass's own prediction**: doesn't meet "not worse on any held-back session" — rejected. Exactly as flagged before any training happened, 300 composites built from only two clean-ish backgrounds can't teach the finder to ignore real clutter it's never seen, and training on them nudged precision down slightly even on easy framings. **Confirms, independently, the conclusion the 4 Nov investigation already reached**: this needs real new photos from Lorcan first — chocolates off the white card (in hand, on a bare/different surface) and genuinely cluttered "none" shots (a keyboard, a hand, a bag, not just empty wood) — not a bigger synthetic batch from what's already on disk. Don't re-attempt the cut-paste approach at any scale until those real photos exist; the pipeline itself (`tools/cutpaste_finder.js`, `training/train_finder.js`) is kept on the `cutpaste-finder` branch, ready to re-run once they do.
